Machine-Written Lean, Measured

An account from the ground up of how AI-written Lean differs from mathlib and from human research code: in its source text, in its compiled proof terms, and in how it names and reuses intermediate results. The core comparison covers OpenAI’s openai/math library, and three further AI corpora are included. A final pass reads the proof terms themselves: how much compression each proof has in it, how much of that its author realizes, and what that suggests about proofs in general.

Summary

Its text reads unlike mathlib’s, mostly in the names.

Under a model of mathlib’s own source, the median OpenAI project costs 7.00 bits per token against 5.15 for a held-out mathlib project (AUC 0.94). 76% of the gap is in identifiers. Human research code sits at 6.67, and its directories written for upstreaming land on mathlib itself (5.15). When OpenAI cites mathlib, it cites commoner lemmas than mathlib does.

Its compiled proofs use a different mix of inferences.

Read as trees of gestures, OpenAI’s proofs are 280 bits from mathlib’s, against 49 bits for random mathlib project sets of the same size: 5.8× the noise. Human research code is 1.9×. OpenAI’s excess lies in arithmetic and normalization certificates; its deficit lies in hand-written logical bookkeeping.

It states many more intermediate claims, and they add size rather than save it.

47% of OpenAI proofs contain a cut, against 17% in mathlib. They are not more compressible (χ 0.035 vs 0.029 bits). They are more redundant: ρ 0.071 vs 0.015 bits (AUC 0.95). 40% of its proofs are larger with their cuts than without them.

Controlling for that layout sharpens some findings and weakens others.

On proofs in optimal cut layout the divergence is still 4.6× the null, and OpenAI’s compiled energy rises above mathlib’s (AUC 0.63), where before it was tied. The first eigenproof, omega certificates inside equation chains, is mostly carried by redundant have claims.

Other AI systems share the redundant layout.

Every AI corpus measured so far has layout redundancy far above mathlib’s and human code’s. The competition provers share OpenAI’s first eigenproof, more strongly than OpenAI: 39% of AxiomProver’s Putnam theorems score beyond +2 SD on it, against 6% of OpenAI’s and 0.7% of mathlib’s. Sphere packing, grown by Gauss from a human base, is near human code in its gesture mix (1.4× its null).

Authors realize different shares of the compression their proofs contain.

Measured on the proof terms themselves, mathlib’s written cuts realize 73% of the sharing an explicit layout finds, OpenAI’s 31%. Compression is intrinsic to a proof, and κ is exactly the log of the average number of times cut elimination copies a step. In every corpus it is small, and the blowup-capable kind of cut, a named implication applied to a proof, is rare.

Corpora and pipeline

Every corpus is measured three ways. The text pass reads the Lean source and needs no compilation. The compiled pass elaborates each proof against mathlib, using OpenAI’s pinned revision built on this machine, and walks the resulting proof term. The cut pass normalizes the same terms several ways and measures exact sizes. Projects, not theorems, are the unit of comparison throughout: a project is a second-level directory (OAI/Area/Topic, Mathlib/A/B). Each group comparison uses projects with at least 20 theorems.

The OpenAI library has 26 million lines, too many to compile on an 8 GB machine, so the compiled pass uses a random draw of 96 projects. To check how much compilation mode matters, 9 of them were also compiled module by module. That gave the same gesture multiset for 97.2% of 4,089 theorems, and the divergence moved from 280.4 to 280.5 bits.

Units

Every measure here is in bits: a description length, or a log-ratio of sizes. Bits from different units are not interchangeable.

Text energy

Fit a 5-gram Kneser–Ney model to mathlib’s Lean source, with identifiers split into mathlib’s naming pieces, and score each declaration by the bits it costs: E(x) = −log₂ q(x). This is a description length. A proof written the way mathlib writes proofs is cheap, and every departure costs bits. Words mathlib has never used are spelled out once, then charged a small reuse cost. Long numerals are booked separately, so numerical certificates cannot dominate. Each mathlib project is scored by a model fitted to the other four fifths of mathlib.

The OpenAI distribution barely overlaps mathlib’s: a random OpenAI project costs more than a random mathlib project 94% of the time. It is not a matter of subject mix. In every one of the 17 areas where both libraries have three or more projects, OpenAI’s median is higher. Against its own area, the typical OpenAI project pays +1.53 bits per token. Split by token kind, 76% of the pooled gap (1.83 bits per token) is in names: identifier pieces mathlib finds unlikely, and words it has never seen. Syntax adds only 0.28.

Against human research code the gap is much smaller (+0.33 bits per token, AUC 0.74), and the subject-matched excess is not significant (q = 0.08). Two differences persist. OpenAI cites its own constants more (+23 points, AUC 0.96). When it cites mathlib, it picks commoner lemmas: library surprisal is -0.93 bits per constant against mathlib.

Compiled proofs

Each elaborated proof term is walked from the root. Every node becomes a gesture: the rule applied, with names erased and types cut to their head connective, such as core:Eq.trans(=,=)⇒= or tac:IntArith(≤)⇒=. Each hypothesis binder is labelled with how often its variable is used: 0 (weakening), 1 (linear) or ω (contraction). These are the resource classes of linear logic, read off the term.

Compiled energy is the text-energy idea moved onto this tree: bits per step under a model of each gesture given its parent. Over all theorems the median OpenAI project costs 3.27 bits per step against mathlib’s 3.26 (AUC 0.53). Theorem by theorem, OpenAI is costlier (3.47 vs 2.83). The re-check below shows the project-level tie comes from redundant claims, which are cheap steps. Text and compiled energy barely track each other across projects (Spearman ρ = 0.14 within mathlib). Holding compiled energy fixed leaves 1.62 of the 1.75-bit text gap.

Structural rules. The resource labels give project-level rates. Against mathlib, OpenAI proofs are longer, state more claims and leave more hypotheses unused. Against the human research code, only the extra claims stand out. Unused hypotheses and dead claims are as common in Carleson and sphere eversion, so they look like features of research formalization in general.

Cut compression

A cut is a proof that is named and then used: a have, a let of a proof, or a β-redex (fun h ↦ q) p. Eliminating it substitutes p for each use of h, and the resource class sets the cost. A dead cut is deleted. A linear cut moves p to its single use. A shared cut copies p, and only a shared cut saves anything. Each proof was normalized five ways, with exact tree sizes: as written, dead cuts removed, linear cuts removed, all cuts removed, and an optimal layout that keeps exactly the cuts that pay for themselves.

The unit is the bit of cut compression. κ = log₂(cut-free size ÷ written size) splits as κ = σ − ℓ − w. Here σ is the sharing gain, ℓ the cost of naming proofs used once and w the cost of proofs never used. κ depends on where the author put the cuts, so two intrinsic quantities sit beside it. χ is the cut-free size against the optimal layout: what the proof’s sharing could save at best. ρ is the written size against that optimum: pure layout redundancy. Then κ = χ − ρ. The same unit applies one level up, with a project’s local lemmas as cuts and citations as uses.

Compression inside proofs is small everywhere: χ ≥ 1 (a doubling) in at most 2.3% of proofs. A few mathlib proofs are chains of hundreds of nested β-redexes whose cut-free forms are 2300 to 2450 times larger, so corpus figures are means of per-proof bits. The human research code is the most compressible (OpenAI vs human, χ AUC 0.22, p = 9·10-4). OpenAI is the most redundant among the three core corpora (ρ AUC 0.95 against mathlib, 0.85 against human; p = 2·10-44 and 4·10-5). Almost all of that is linear overhead: single-use claims. At the lemma level, inlining every local lemma multiplies the median project by 21.1 in mathlib and 21.5 in OpenAI, a difference that is not significant (p = 0.11).

Eigenproofs

Each corpus becomes a cloud of square-root gesture-mix vectors over the 300 commonest gestures, with every project weighted equally and both covariances lightly shrunk. The generalized eigenvectors of OpenAI’s covariance against mathlib’s are the directions along which OpenAI varies unlike mathlib. Under a Gaussian approximation the KL divergence splits exactly across them, so each eigenproof carries bits. OpenAI’s total is 280 bits; random sets of 96 mathlib projects give 49 (at most 58 in ten draws). Bars below are OpenAI’s eigenproofs; the solid tick is the null’s median eigenvalue at that rank and the dashed tick its maximum.

The first eigenproof belongs to OpenAI: 6.3% of its theorems score beyond +2 mathlib SD, against 0.7% in mathlib and 0.5% in human code. The others are habits OpenAI shares with human research code, usually to a similar degree. Split-half recovery of the first direction is 0.95. mathlib has switched from omega to lia and OpenAI has not; the two are merged here, and kept apart the total rises to 316 bits.

Layout re-check

OpenAI writes many redundant claims, and each one adds steps and gestures, so any earlier difference could be layout and nothing more. Every compiled analysis was therefore rerun on theorems in optimal cut layout (ρ = 0) and on theorems with no cut at all. Definitions carry no cut measure, so a run on proofs only is the control. The optimal-layout subset keeps 60% of OpenAI’s proofs, 89% of mathlib’s and 66% of the human code’s.

The divergence is not layout. On optimal-layout proofs it is 4.6× the null (proofs only: 5.5×). Comparing each stratum only with the same stratum of mathlib, in the original basis, gives 342 bits for optimal-layout proofs and 209 for redundant ones.

Eigenproof 1 is mostly layout. 76% of OpenAI’s excess on it comes from proofs with redundant layout, which are 40% of its proofs. The pattern is have h : a = b := by omega. Rebuilt on optimal-layout proofs, the integer-arithmetic direction drops to rank 5 (13 bits), and the top direction becomes dependent rewriting under binders (46 bits), whose prevalence does not depend on layout. Even on optimal-layout proofs, OpenAI scores beyond +2 on eigenproofs 2–8 13 to 31 times as often as mathlib.

Compiled energy changes. On optimal-layout proofs OpenAI’s median project costs 3.37 bits per step against 3.13 (AUC 0.63, q < 0.001). The redundant claims are cheap, predictable steps that pulled its average down. The text gap does not move (1.75 → 1.78 bits per token).

Other AI corpora

The same pipeline was run on other AI-written Lean, each compiled against the mathlib used here with its own lakefile settings. Projects written for older Lean versions lose theorems to version drift, and failed theorems are dropped. That is a survivorship filter, since longer proofs are likelier to break. The IMO corpus keeps only a third of its theorems, so its column is indicative at best. Divergences use the corpus’s own size-matched null, and small corpora get a large null.

Redundant layout is common to all of them. Sphere packing and Putnam have higher ρ than OpenAI (0.189 and 0.174 bits, AUC 0.95 and 1.00 against mathlib projects), and neither is more compressible than mathlib. The competition provers carry OpenAI’s first eigenproof. 39% of AxiomProver’s Putnam theorems and 26% of Aristotle’s IMO theorems score beyond +2 on it. Their own most divergent direction is the same omega-in-equation-chain pattern (match 0.95 to OpenAI’s). Sphere packing looks like human research code in its gesture mix (1.4× its null; its top direction is dependent rewriting, the habit OpenAI shares with human code), but it has the most redundant layout of any corpus. In text, all three sit between mathlib and OpenAI, near human research code.

Proof terms

The cut measures above rank layouts against one another. The question they leave open is how much compression a proof has in it at all, whoever laid it out. This pass reads the proof term itself. It eliminates every written cut by substitution, without ever building the result. A cut variable is hashed as the proof it stands for, and an ordinary bound variable as its binder. So the same subproof is one node wherever it occurs, whether it was written twice, named once and used twice, or substituted from a cut. Three quantities come out, all in bits of cut compression against the same cut-free size S:

  • χauthor = log₂(S ÷ the written cuts in their optimal layout): what the author’s own cuts achieve.
  • χlb = log₂(S ÷ G), where G is the exact size of an explicit layout. It names any repeated subproof that pays for its own name, cut or not, and is refined greedily. Any layout is a lower bound on the best, χ*.
  • χDAG = log₂(S ÷ D), where D counts distinct subproofs. That is sharing with no naming cost, an upper bound on χ*.

So χauthor ≤ χ* and χlb ≤ χ* ≤ χDAG. The share an author realizes is Σχauthor ÷ Σχlb, a conservative estimate since χlb ≤ χ*. A subproof is shared only with an identical one whose free variables are bound by the same binders, so every node G names could be a have at that point. Parametrized lemmas, which abstract over a varying part, are not counted. Before it was run on any corpus, the pass was checked on hand-computed theorems (eigen/tests/terms_toy). Its cut-free size agrees with the cut pass’s exact count for 98.5% of mathlib proofs. The exceptions are proofs with created redexes, which this pass leaves unreduced.

Authors realize very different shares. mathlib’s cuts realize 73% of the compression the explicit layout finds, OpenAI’s 31%. Human research: 61%. Sphere packing: 14%. Putnam 2025: 30%. IMO 2025: 33%. Sphere packing, closest to human code in its gesture mix, realizes the least. How much there is to realize depends mostly on proof length. The corpus means of χDAG differ by factors of 2 to 4, but from 100 nodes up the size bins agree within a factor of about 1.6, so AI proofs have more mostly because they are longer. The realization gap is not a length effect: in each size bin from 30 nodes up, mathlib realizes more (second table below). Across projects, OpenAI against mathlib gives AUC 0.25 (p = 6·10-15). The AI corpora pay twice. They write cuts that save nothing, the layout redundancy ρ above, and they leave out cuts that would save something. In mathlib the misses are concentrated in a few computational proofs. Across all its proofs of 10 or more steps, the written cuts cut the cut-free size by 36%, the explicit layout by 48%, and full sharing by 55%. The largest gaps are brute-force determinants and coordinate formulas, where tactics emit the same subproof many times.

Logical rank. Each cut’s formula gets a rank: the depth of implications nested to the left, with ¬A read as A → ⊥ and definitions unfolded, so s ⊆ t or Monotone f count as implications. In mathlib 20% of cuts have rank ≥ 1, about double the purely syntactic count (8%). A created redex appears when cut elimination substitutes a λ-proof into a position where it is applied to a proof. It is a new cut that complete development does not remove. These occur in 0.8% of mathlib proofs and 1.4% of OpenAI’s.

Reproducibility. 2,879 OpenAI theorems were compiled both in one shared environment and module by module (auxiliary _proof_n lemmas, numbered differently in the two modes, are set aside). 99.8% have identical written size, cut-free size and DAG. The 5 that differ do so outside their cuts: written and cut-free size move by the same few nodes, such as an instance found by a different path. So compilation mode never moves the cuts of a proof without changing the proof. That layout does not affect χ* follows from the construction, and is checked on hand-built pairs.

What it suggests

The measurements support four statements about proofs in general. Two are theorems with short proofs, one is classical, and one is empirical.

Copy-count identity

Proved; checked on every proof

Eliminate every written cut. A written step x is copied m(x) times, where m sums, over the uses of each cut whose proof contains x, the copies of that use. Then the cut-free size is Σx m(x), less the binder and use nodes that disappear. So κ is log₂ of the average copy count of a written step, and one bit of cut compression is a doubling of it. Two corollaries hold for every proof in every corpus. First, κ ≤ Σcuts log₂ uses, since m(x) is at most the product of the use counts of the cuts enclosing x. Eliminating outermost cuts first, each step multiplies size by at most its use count. Second, χDAG = log₂ of the average occurrence count of a distinct subproof, which is at most log₂ of the largest. The product bound is loose, reaching a median 0.18 of itself in mathlib, because it ignores how large the copied proofs are. The first-order version, Σ log₂(1 + (uses − 1)·|p|/N), exact for a single cut, tracks κ with Spearman 0.95.

Compression is intrinsic

By construction; checked on hand-built pairs

S, D and χ* depend only on the cut-free proof, so any two layouts of one proof have the same χ*, and κ = χ − ρ separates the proof from its layout. have h := p; ⟨h, h⟩ and ⟨p, p⟩ get identical S and D, and compiling 2,879 theorems two ways reproduces every measure except where the proof itself changed. Compression becomes a property of the mathematics, and layout a property of the author.

Rank ladder

Classical; confirmed on the data

A created redex that applies a substituted proof to a proof needs a cut whose formula, unfolded, has rank ≥ 1. Rank-0 cuts can only create instantiations with data. So with rank-0 cuts, eliminating cuts costs at most the single-exponential product bound above. The tower-of-exponentials blowups of cut elimination (Statman; Orevkov) need cuts of positive rank, and the classical upper bound is a tower whose height grows with rank. The implication held for 99.6% of the 1,100 mathlib proofs with such redexes (of the 4 left, the one inspected by hand has a hypothesis that is a proposition only through a defined family, R.arrows f for a sieve, which even the unfolding pass reads as data; the others are not yet explained). Before definitions were unfolded and cuts behind data β-redexes were seen, it seemed to fail for over half. That was a measurement error, found by printing a counterexample.

Real proofs sit in the elementary regime

Empirical

χDAG ≥ 1 bit (sharing could halve the proof) in 2.1% of mathlib proofs; created proof redexes are rare; most cuts are rank 0. Libraries use cuts to organize proofs, not to compress them: what cut elimination could cost them in theory is not what it costs them in practice. The exceptions are computational: certificates and expanded polynomial identities.

Directions

  • Is the optimal layout tractable? With only exact, scope-respecting sharing, choosing which subproofs to name is an optimization over the DAG with a naming cost. The greedy layout here is sandwiched against the DAG. Is χ* polynomial-time, or hard like the smallest-grammar problem it resembles once parametrized lemmas are allowed? The gap χDAG − χlb measured here bounds how much an exact algorithm could gain.
  • The distribution of copy counts. By the identity, κ and χDAG are log-means of copy counts. Their tails, which are a few proofs with enormous multiplicity, decide every corpus mean. A model of where multiplicity comes from (tactics, automation, author habit) would predict compression from the proof script alone.
  • Rank as a resource. Only cuts of positive rank allow large speed-ups. In mathlib, though, proofs whose shared cuts reach rank 1 or more have less compression available than rank-0 ones (χDAG 0.50–0.60 against 0.74). What rises with rank is created redexes, from 5% to 32%: a shared implication is a local lemma that gets applied, which is abstraction, not repetition. Whether hard proofs in other fields use rank for compression, as the theory allows, can be tested on bigger corpora.
  • A layout optimizer. For AI-written proofs the two inefficiencies are separate and both mechanical. Dropping redundant cuts and naming repeated subproofs are term-level rewrites that keep the theorem. The bounds here say how much each could save per corpus before anyone writes one.

Method and limits

  • Elaboration. Lean 4.34.1, mathlib d13f23b. OpenAI projects were compiled in one shared environment, module by module. The human baseline was compiled module by module against its own imports, as lake would. Carleson’s checkout predates the mathlib pin, so 222 of its 4,898 theorems fail and are dropped. Theorems containing sorryAx are dropped everywhere. Auxiliary theorems (_proof_n, equation lemmas) are folded into their source.
  • Text model. Interpolated Kneser–Ney, order 5, chosen on held-out mathlib cross-entropy. Unknown pieces are spelled with a character model, so energy is a proper code length. Up to 1,000 declarations per non-mathlib project are sampled.
  • Statistics. Medians with bootstrap intervals; two-sided Mann–Whitney AUC; Benjamini–Hochberg q across tests. Eigenproofs use theorems with at least 10 steps, project-balanced weights and covariance shrinkage α = 0.1. The decomposition is stable across shrinkage choices for the first four directions.
  • Cuts. Use counts are proof-position occurrences. A proof used only inside a type or data stays alive as one copy. Sizes are exact big integers. The optimal layout decides bottom-up, eliminating a cut when (uses − 1)·|p| ≤ uses + 1.
  • Proof terms. Cut-free form computed through a substitution environment with canonical hashes, so it is never built. A cut variable hashes as its proof, an ordinary binder by its domain and enclosing binder. Data β-redexes are substituted silently, created redexes are left in place and counted (identical ones once). The explicit layout names a node when references × size exceed size + references + 1, and is then refined greedily up to six times. Budgets: 200k DAG nodes and 400k rank steps per proof; proofs over budget are dropped (10 of 254,147 in mathlib). Rank unfolds definitions up to 24 times, reducing projections of constructor applications.

Limits.

  • Low energy means “reads like mathlib”, not “is good mathematics”. mathlib is one community’s house style, and research code, human or AI, is expected to coin vocabulary.
  • The compiled OpenAI sample is 96 projects of about 524; the text pass covers them all.
  • The human baseline is small: 11 compiled projects from two efforts. Its intervals are wide, and its null is large.
  • Other AI corpora were compiled against a newer mathlib than they were written for. Failures are not random.
  • χ* is bracketed, not computed, and sharing is exact: a lemma abstracted over a varying part is not found.
  • Every pass decides whether a term is a proof from the declared type of its statement’s head symbol. A statement that is a proposition only once definitions unfold, such as S x for S : Set α written as an application, is treated as data, so its have is not counted as a cut. The third pass counts these: 0.03% of mathlib proofs have one, 0.13 for every 100 cuts counted. The same rule applies to every corpus. Rank uses the unfolding test.
  • A 5-gram model is a weak model of Lean. A stronger one would lower every energy; whether it would close the gaps is untested.
